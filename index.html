About the work

Abstract

Dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands is the next frontier of robotics, yet learned hand skills remain limited to simple grasps or repeated motions. As robots attempt more demanding in-hand maneuvers, safety—such as not dropping the object—becomes critical, yet it is rarely measured.

We study in-hand object pose reaching: moving an object to any position and orientation relative to the hand, at any wrist orientation, through long sequences of consecutive goals. We present a simulation-trained recipe for a LEAP hand that combines generated grasp datasets, diverse resets and goals, an adaptive friction curriculum, careful system identification, and teacher–student policies that use either motion capture or a single wrist-mounted depth camera.

A learned residual safety filter sits on top of the task policy and exposes adjustable conservativeness at deployment. Across 13.3 hours of real-robot evaluation and 4,800 unseen goals, the state policy reaches 95% of goals within 2 cm and 0.4 rad; the filter cuts drops by 75% at a cost of 3.7 points of success.

Beyond the benchmark

One portable skill, many difficult behaviors.

The wrist-camera policy reaches out-of-distribution goals, withstands fast arm motion and physical perturbations, and composes with arm motion for longer tasks—including flipping a wineglass, reorienting a rubber duck, and screwing in a lightbulb until it turns on.
